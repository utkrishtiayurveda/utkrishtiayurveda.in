PCOS / PCOD: Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis & a Practical Care Approach

PCOS is more than an “ovarian cyst” problem. It can affect menstrual cycles, ovulation, skin and hair, fertility, metabolism and emotional wellbeing—and the pattern can be different for every person.

4 September 202610 min readUtkrishti Ayurveda, Noida Extension
PCOS / PCODUnderstanding the condition before choosing care

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), commonly also called PCOD in everyday conversation, is a common hormonal and metabolic condition. The World Health Organization estimates that it affects about 10–13% of reproductive-aged women, while many affected people remain undiagnosed.

At a glance

PCOS can involve irregular periods or ovulation, signs of higher androgen activity such as acne or excess hair, and metabolic concerns such as insulin resistance. Having “cysts” on an ultrasound is not required for every PCOS diagnosis.

What is PCOS?

PCOS affects the way reproductive hormones and metabolism function. It may become noticeable in adolescence, during reproductive years, or when someone seeks help for irregular cycles or difficulty conceiving. Despite the name, PCOS is not defined simply by ovarian cysts.

The condition is chronic, but symptoms and health risks can be managed. Care should focus not only on periods or fertility but also on longer-term metabolic, cardiovascular and emotional health.

PCOS vs PCOD: are they different?

“PCOD” is widely used in India, but major international evidence-based guidance uses the term polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). On this website we use “PCOS / PCOD” because patients commonly search and speak using both terms; when discussing diagnosis and evidence, PCOS is the standard term.

Common symptoms of PCOS

Symptoms vary considerably. A person may have only some of these features, and their pattern may change over time.

  • Irregular, infrequent or absent menstrual periods
  • Difficulty with ovulation or difficulty conceiving
  • Acne or oily skin
  • Excess facial or body hair
  • Hair thinning or female-pattern hair loss
  • Weight gain or difficulty managing weight in some people
  • Possible metabolic concerns such as insulin resistance

PCOS can also affect quality of life and may be associated with anxiety, depression, body-image concerns or disordered eating. These concerns deserve the same respect as physical symptoms.

Why does PCOS happen?

There is no single known cause. Genetics, hormonal signalling and metabolic factors can all play a role. A family history of PCOS or type 2 diabetes may increase risk. This is why reducing PCOS to one food, one hormone or one lifestyle habit can be misleading.

How is PCOS diagnosed?

Diagnosis should be made by an appropriately qualified healthcare professional after considering symptoms and excluding other possible causes. In adults, commonly used criteria look for at least two of three broad features: signs or biochemical evidence of higher androgen activity, irregular or absent ovulation/menstrual cycles, and polycystic ovarian morphology. Current international guidance also discusses anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) as an alternative to ultrasound for defining polycystic ovarian morphology in adults in appropriate diagnostic pathways.

An ultrasound alone does not establish PCOS, and some people with PCOS do not have polycystic-appearing ovaries. Blood tests or other evaluation may be needed depending on the clinical picture.

PCOS and fertility

PCOS is a common cause of problems with ovulation and can contribute to infertility, but having PCOS does not mean pregnancy is impossible. Fertility assessment and treatment depend on age, ovulation, duration of trying to conceive, partner factors and other reproductive health considerations.

If pregnancy is a goal, it is better to discuss the complete fertility picture rather than assuming PCOS is the only possible factor.

Why long-term health matters

PCOS is associated with higher risks of insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes and several cardiometabolic concerns. Irregular or very infrequent periods can also matter for endometrial health. Sleep and psychological wellbeing may need attention too. Follow-up therefore should not focus only on making periods appear regular.

Lifestyle is part of care—not a punishment

Healthy eating, physical activity, adequate sleep and sustainable daily habits are important for people with PCOS, including when weight loss is not the goal or does not occur. The 2023 International Evidence-based PCOS Guideline emphasises healthy lifestyle, emotional wellbeing, quality of life and awareness of weight stigma.

A useful plan is one a person can actually follow. Extreme diets, fear-based food rules and promises to “reverse PCOS permanently” should be treated cautiously.

When should you seek medical advice?

Consider professional evaluation if periods are persistently irregular or absent, symptoms such as excess hair or acne are significant, you are concerned about blood sugar/metabolic health, or you are having difficulty conceiving. Seek urgent medical care for severe pain, very heavy bleeding, fainting, or other acute or rapidly worsening symptoms.
